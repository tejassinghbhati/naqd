01

The underlying is a coin flip

Every market asks the same question: does the asset close at or above where the window opened? Across every resolved market on the venue, it does almost exactly half the time. There is no drift to harvest here - so any edge has to come from the price being wrong, not from the asset going up.

02

Prices are informative, but miscalibrated

Plotting what the venue charged against what actually happened gives a calibration curve. Perfect pricing lies on the diagonal. This venue is well clear of a coin flip - but it bends away from the diagonal at both ends: cheap markets resolve even less often than their price implies, expensive ones more.

!
Most of that curve is the clock, not skill. Scoring a market by its volume-weighted price mixes trades from the whole window together - including ones placed seconds before expiry, when the outcome is nearly decided. Score each trade separately by how much of the window was left and the curve flattens sharply. The check below is what separates a real edge from an artifact of aggregation.
03

But the edge is not constant - and that changes everything

Here is the result the rest of this project is built on. Measure the average pricing error three ways and you get three different answers, ranging from "obviously real" to "indistinguishable from noise." Only the last one is honest.

04

Most markets never trade at all

The venue's real bottleneck is not signal quality, it is emptiness. Most event contracts are listed, run their window and settle without a single position being taken. That is where a quote is worth the most - and it is why the agent's job is to provide liquidity, not to take it.

Who gets paid

Settled PnL split by which side of the trade a wallet was on. The venue charges no fees, so this is purely the spread changing hands.

05

What the agent does about it

The agent's default state is flat. It takes positive evidence to make it quote, and all four of these must hold on every pass:

  1. Enough resolved markets in the lookback window to estimate anything at all.
  2. The window's confidence interval excludes zero - there is a measurable mispricing.
  3. It is larger than the minimum edge, so we are not trading noise into a spread.
  4. Its sign agrees with the long-run estimate. Condition 2 alone fires by chance about one window in twenty; this is what separates a regime from a run of luck.

When it does quote, it quotes post-only on both sides around an edge-corrected fair value, sizes to the near bound of the interval rather than its centre, refuses markets in the last stretch of their window, and never posts inside the touch. Order expiry is set just past the requote interval, so a crashed agent's orders age off the book by themselves.

The API underneath

DreamDEX's own HTTP API covers spot only, so anything wanting event-contract data today has to run the TypeScript SDK. Every number on this page is also available as plain JSON, no key required.
